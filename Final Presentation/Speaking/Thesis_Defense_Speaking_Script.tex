\section*{Master Thesis Presentation}
\begin{itemize}
\item Good afternoon, my name is Heykel Khadhraoui. I developed a surface grinding controller for a seven-joint robot.
\item Normal pressing and compliant rotation allow contact to move the tool towards alignment. A separate experiment tests how the arm responds to disturbances.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Motivation}
\begin{itemize}
\item Grinding needs the tool face aligned with the physical surface. The configured and physical surface orientations have an unknown difference.
\item With a tilted tool, normal pressing creates a contact moment. Low rotational stiffness lets this moment turn the tool towards alignment.
\item Cartesian impedance makes position and rotation compliant. The desired orientation stays fixed while the rotational spring and damper remain active.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Overview}
\begin{itemize}
\item I will explain the controller, introduce the experiments, and present the validation and results. I will finish with conclusions and future work.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Cartesian pose}
\begin{itemize}
\item The end effector (EE) is the tool or gripper at the end of the arm. Its Cartesian pose describes position and rotation relative to the robot base frame.
\item Position uses x, y, and z, while roll, pitch, and yaw illustrate rotation. Together they give six degrees of freedom.
\item Forward kinematics calculates the pose from all seven joint angles, with \(p=p(q)\) for position. The controller uses a rotation matrix for orientation.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Cartesian impedance controller}
\begin{itemize}
\item Stiffness acts like a spring: for the same error, higher stiffness gives a larger restoring force or moment.
\item Position errors generate forces, and orientation errors generate moments. Damping opposes differences from the desired linear and angular velocities.
\item Force and moment form a wrench, controlled by six-by-six matrices with three-by-three blocks. The zero off-diagonal blocks mean translation and rotation are decoupled here.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Real-time control}
\begin{itemize}
\item The robot receives joint torques. We generate the Cartesian contribution from the wrench using the Jacobian transpose:
\[
\tau_{\mathrm{cart}}=J^\top(q)F
\]
\item The Jacobian maps joint velocities to the EE's linear and angular velocities. Model compensation and enabled null-space torques complete the command.
\item The measured pose feeds back into the control loop. The C++ controller uses libfranka and updates every millisecond.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Translation–rotation coupling}
\begin{itemize}
\item The tool centre point (TCP) is the controlled reference point attached to the EE. The centre of compliance (CoC) at \(p_c\) is a virtual reference for stiffness and damping.
\item Moving the CoC changes how position and rotation errors generate force and moment. Its displacement from the TCP is \(r_c\):
\[
r_c=p_c-p_{\mathrm{TCP}}
\]
\item For small rotations, we express the shifted stiffness and damping at the TCP. Non-zero off-diagonal blocks show the coupling:
\[
\begin{aligned}K_{\mathrm{TCP}}&=A^\top K_cA,\\D_{\mathrm{TCP}}&=A^\top D_cA.\end{aligned}
\]
\end{itemize}

\section*{Centre of compliance (CoC)}
\begin{itemize}
\item Shifting the CoC can change both the commanded force and moment. The shifted controller's force acts as if applied at \(p_c\):
\[
m=m_R+r_c\times f
\]
\item The added moment can support or oppose alignment. For the same force, reversing the displacement reverses this contribution.
\item With the CoC at the TCP, the added contribution vanishes. The rotational spring and damper still produce \(m_R\).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Null-space controller}
\begin{itemize}
\item Alongside the tool response, we control the arm's extra motion. At full Jacobian rank, seven joints leave one null-space direction with zero instantaneous EE motion:
\[
J(q)\dot q_{\mathrm{null}}=0
\]
\item Damping opposes this redundant joint motion.
\item A second term seeks easier motion in the weakest EE direction. This objective is called conditioning and can initiate arm motion.
\item At a singularity, the Jacobian loses rank and the EE loses an instantaneous motion direction. Near it, some EE motions require very large joint velocities.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Contact experiment}
\begin{itemize}
\item The process goes from tool orientation and surface approach to contact establishment, then grinding. These experiments evaluate the five-second contact-establishment phase.
\item Low normal stiffness allows compliant pressing, while higher tangential stiffness resists sideways movement. Rotational stiffness is higher about the normal and lower about the surface tangents.
\item The position reference advances into the surface to generate pressing force. The desired orientation stays at its entry value, so contact moments drive the rotation.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Angular quantities}
\begin{itemize}
\item The measured angular offset compares the entry orientation with the configured surface. It uses measured EE orientation and the calibrated tool normal.
\item The contact response is defined from the measured end orientation back to entry. Its sign is therefore opposite to the EE's actual start-to-end rotation.
\item Positive rotation is anticlockwise in the sketch, and an aligning response has the same sign as the offset. These quantities do not directly measure the physical tool--surface angle.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Null-space experiment}
\begin{itemize}
\item The desired position and orientation stay fixed while joint torques apply a repeatable disturbance. This is equivalent to a virtual 20-newton point force on link three.
\item We compare no null-space torque, damping, and two conditioning settings, with three trials each. Conditioning is already active during the five seconds before the disturbance.
\item All results use seconds five to nine, displayed as zero to four after disturbance onset. We compare total projected joint motion, minimum singular value, and net projected displacement.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Normal-force plausibility assessment}
\begin{itemize}
\item I manually push the tool about 20 millimetres along the surface normal, with the CoC at the TCP. This checks the spring-force prediction with the desired pose fixed.
\item The measured normal position-error change is \(-19.65\) millimetres. The spring predicts \(-19.65\) newtons, closely matching the commanded \(-19.64\) newtons.
\item The robot's model estimate is \(-22.292\) newtons, a 13.50 percent difference from the command.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Moment plausibility assessment}
\begin{itemize}
\item I manually rotate the tool about surface tangent one while keeping the normal push. This checks the spring-moment prediction with the desired pose fixed and CoC at the TCP.
\item The measured rotational error changes by \(6.565^\circ\), about six and a half degrees. We subtract the moment already present under the normal push.
\item The predicted and commanded moments are both 1.719 newton metres. The model estimate is 1.852 newton metres, about 7.75 percent higher.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Commanded and estimated wrench}
\begin{itemize}
\item A separate contact-establishment trial compares commanded force and moment with the robot's model estimates. The CoC is at the TCP.
\item During the final stationary interval, mean differences are 2.32 percent for force and 4.87 percent for moment. The curves differ more during motion.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Baseline contact response}
\begin{itemize}
\item With the CoC at the TCP, both larger entry offsets produced EE rotation towards the configured surface. The nominal-zero condition showed only a small response.
\item Entry offsets of \(+9.31^\circ\) and \(-9.41^\circ\) give responses of \(+7.57^\circ\) and \(-10.83^\circ\).
\item The contact comparisons show three-trial means. Physical alignment is assessed indirectly from EE rotation.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Effect of stiffness}
\begin{itemize}
\item Higher rotational stiffness opposes contact-induced rotation. Increasing it from 5 to 50 newton metres per radian reduced the response by about 64 percent.
\item Across the tested tangential stiffness range, the response varied by only \(0.03^\circ\). Only one tangent was varied, while the other stayed fixed.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Effect of CoC position}
\begin{itemize}
\item For a positive entry offset, plus 40 millimetres supports contact rotation and minus 40 millimetres nearly suppresses it.
\item For a negative entry offset, the supporting displacement reverses.
\item In the tested conditions, supporting displacement increases the response by about 4 percent. Opposing displacement reduces it by about 98 to 99 percent.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Contact response and interaction wrench}
\begin{itemize}
\item In these traces, contact rotation settles in about 2.3 seconds with supporting CoC displacement, compared with 3.6 seconds at the TCP.
\item Final estimated normal forces are similar, at about \(-83\), \(-79\), and \(-78\) newtons.
\item The interaction moment reverses with CoC displacement. These forces and moments come from the robot's model estimate.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Cumulative null-space motion}
\begin{itemize}
\item Cumulative motion adds projected joint movement in both directions. These degrees describe arm motion, not rotation of the tool.
\item Damping reduces it from \(7.598^\circ\) to \(5.689^\circ\), about 25.1 percent. Conditioning gives \(0.288^\circ\) at \(k_\sigma=1.5\) and \(1.687^\circ\) at \(k_\sigma=2\).
\item Curves show three-trial means. Shading shows one sample standard deviation.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Jacobian conditioning}
\begin{itemize}
\item A smaller minimum singular value means some tool motions require larger joint velocities. Zero means the EE loses an instantaneous motion direction.
\item It falls with no null-space torque and with damping. Both conditioning settings keep it nearly constant during the disturbance.
\item Curves show three-trial means using the same Jacobian convention. Shading shows one sample standard deviation.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Net null-space displacement}
\begin{itemize}
\item Net displacement measures projected joint movement remaining after opposite motions cancel. It is \(7.516^\circ\) without null-space torque and \(5.609^\circ\) with damping.
\item Mean conditioning displacements are only \(0.015^\circ\) and \(-0.011^\circ\). Conditioning counters the disturbance in the tested direction.
\item Cumulative motions are \(0.288^\circ\) and \(1.687^\circ\). The larger value is consistent with more back-and-forth motion despite similarly small net displacement.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Conclusion}
\begin{itemize}
\item Contact drove EE rotation towards alignment while the desired orientation stayed fixed. Physical alignment was assessed indirectly.
\item Within the tested rotational stiffness range, higher stiffness reduced contact rotation by about 64 percent.
\item Tested tangential stiffness changes had little effect: only \(0.03^\circ\) variation. The other tangent remained fixed.
\item CoC displacement supports or opposes rotation. At the TCP, its added moment vanishes while the rotational spring and damper remain active.
\item Damping reduced arm motion, and conditioning countered the tested disturbance direction. Nearly zero net displacement coexisted with back-and-forth motion.
\item Future work is a more rigid tool mount and adaptive CoC selection. After alignment, the adaptive CoC should return to the TCP.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Null-space torques and projector}
\begin{itemize}
\item The kinematic projector selects null-space torque directions. It uses the Jacobian pseudoinverse \(J^+\).
\[
N_\tau=I_7-J^\top J^{+\top}
\]
\item Damping and conditioning add to give the total null-space torque.
\[
\tau_{\mathrm{null}}=\tau_d+\tau_\sigma
\]
\item Damping opposes projected joint velocity and is zero at rest. \(d_{\mathrm{null}}\) is the damping coefficient.
\[
\tau_d=-d_{\mathrm{null}}N_\tau\dot q
\]
\item Conditioning probes both directions along the unit null-space vector \(v_7\). It chooses the direction with the larger minimum singular value.
\[
\tau_\sigma=k_\sigma N_\tau(s_\sigma v_7)
\]
\item The selector \(s_\sigma\) is \(+1\) or \(-1\), and zero within the deadband. \(k_\sigma\) sets the torque magnitude, which can be nonzero at rest.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Disturbance demonstration}
\begin{itemize}

\item Damping opposes the joint motion.
\item Conditioning actively changes the arm configuration and can initiate motion. The tool stays approximately in place.
\end{itemize}
